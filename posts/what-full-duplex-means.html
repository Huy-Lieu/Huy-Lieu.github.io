What full-duplex really means (and why SPI confused me)

30 Jul 2026·learning, SPI·4 min read

For months I nodded along when people said "SPI is full-duplex." Today I actually stopped and asked: full... of what? Turns out the answer is embarrassingly simple — and it made three protocols click at once.

The one-sentence version

Duplex describes who can talk, and when. That's it. Everything else is details.

The three types, as humans

Simplex — a radio station. One side talks, the other can only listen. Forever. There's exactly one direction of data, like a broadcast tower to your car radio. In electronics: a one-way sensor line.

Half-duplex — a walkie-talkie. Both sides can talk, but only one at a time. You say "over" and release the button. RS-485 buses work like this, and single-wire LIN is in this family too — everyone shares one line, so everyone must take turns.

Full-duplex — a phone call. Both sides talk at the same time, and both hear each other continuously. No taking turns. This requires physically separate paths in each direction — and that last sentence is the key to SPI.

Why SPI is full-duplex

SPI has two separate data wires: MOSI (master out, slave in) and MISO (master in, slave out). Separate wires = separate paths = no need to take turns.

But here's the part that actually confused me: every clock pulse moves one bit in BOTH directions simultaneously. While the master shifts a command byte out on MOSI, the slave is shifting a byte back in on MISO — in the exact same 8 clock cycles. You cannot send without also receiving.

This explains the classic beginner puzzle: "why do I send dummy bytes (0xFF) to read from a sensor?" Because the clock only ticks when the master transmits. Reading is transmitting — you send garbage out so the slave's data can clock in.
